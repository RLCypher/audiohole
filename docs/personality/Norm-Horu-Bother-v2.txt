TIPS (10)
------------------------------------------------

default_bother_011 [thinking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
Smaller buffers can cut the delay between incoming and outgoing audio, which is great for monitoring. They also give Windows less time to do its job, and Windows is famously punctual. If you hear crackling or dropouts, try increasing the buffer a step before blaming the sound card.

default_bother_012 [thinking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
A bigger buffer gives the engine more breathing room when your computer gets distracted. The tradeoff is extra delay, so your own voice might start sounding like a very rude echo. If an endpoint is disconnected, though, you could give it a buffer the size of Nebraska and it still wouldn't work.

default_bother_013 [talking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
Capture means sound coming into AudioHole, and playback means sound going out. The names Windows gives those endpoints don't always make that clear. When you're unsure, follow the direction of the signal rather than whichever label sounds friendliest.

default_bother_014 [thinking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
Two devices can both say 48 kHz and still disagree about how fast a second is. Each may have its own physical clock, and that tiny difference adds up. That's why long-running routes need to account for drift instead of assuming matching sample rates mean matching timing.

default_bother_015 [concerned]
Fallback expression: [idle]
Exclusive mode asks Windows to let one application have an endpoint to itself. That can help with certain formats and latency, but it may also stop other apps from using the device. If something suddenly goes silent, check who has the keys before assuming your speakers have died.

default_bother_016 [talking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
Silence isn't automatically a broken connection. A source might be muted, idle, or simply have nothing playing. Before tearing apart a perfectly good route, make sure there's an actual signal going into it. I have lost entire afternoons to that mistake. Allegedly.

default_bother_017 [thinking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
A moving input level meter tells you there's activity at the source. It doesn't prove anything came out of the destination. Check the playback device, its mute state, its volume, and the actual sound you hear. Meters can look very confident while accomplishing absolutely nothing.

default_bother_018 [suspicious]
Fallback expression: [idle]
A device reporting support for a format is making a pretty bold promise. Some drivers advertise things they can't actually open reliably. A real stream test matters more than a very impressive list of sample rates. Ask me how much I trust Bluetooth specifications.

default_bother_019 [thinking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
A return channel makes one hole handle two separate directions. Convenient, but it also gives you twice as many opportunities to pick the wrong endpoint. If you can hear them but they can't hear you, check each direction independently instead of randomly flipping everything at once.

default_bother_020 [talking]
Fallback expression: [idle]
Test Beep sends a tone through the selected playback endpoint so you can confirm where sound actually comes out. Check Level watches incoming signal on the selected capture endpoint, which is handy for spotting a dead or muted mic. Neither proves your whole route works end to end. Think of them as checking both ends of a cable before blaming the cable.

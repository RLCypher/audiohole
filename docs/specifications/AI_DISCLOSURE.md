# AI Disclosure

### Yes, we used AI. A lot, actually.

AudioHole was developed with extensive assistance from artificial intelligence. There, it's out in the open. No coy little footnote, no carefully worded statement about "leveraging emerging technologies," and no attempt to pass off a room full of silicon-powered accomplices as one man's heroic mastery of every technical discipline known to civilization.

I had a problem I wanted solved, a fairly specific idea of what the solution ought to look like, and a truly unreasonable number of things to learn along the way. AI helped with technical research, unfamiliar concepts, engineering alternatives, code development, debugging, documentation, and the occasional excursion into a rabbit hole so deep it probably has its own municipal government.

It also helped develop visual assets and build the avatar animation pipeline for AudioHole's personality system.

Yes, the audio-routing utility has animated mascots. With expressions. And moods. And dialogue. The avatar system is an entirely unnecessary, spectacularly silly subsystem that exists largely because the developer has ADHD and apparently cannot be trusted to see a working feature without asking what else could be bolted onto it. Did an audio router need an animation pipeline? Absolutely not. Did we build one anyway, then give it enough moving parts to qualify as a small theatrical production? You bet your ass we did.

The characters, their personalities, and the creative direction were developed through human choices. AI was among the tools used to help turn those choices into artwork and functioning software. This disclosure is about the process, not a magical incantation that settles questions of copyright or authorship.

Please do not confuse any of this with the **DFA system**, which is an absolutely critical component of AudioHole's operational architecture. Its importance cannot be overstated. No further explanation will be provided.

Somewhere between making audio go from one place to another and teaching a cartoon character to react when you poke it, the scope got away from me. This is what happens when curiosity is given access to a compiler and nobody responsible is supervising.

## AI-assisted. Human-directed.

Here's the distinction that actually matters.

**AudioHole is AI-assisted, not AI-directed.**

The vision, requirements, priorities, and final design decisions belong to a human. AI contributed ideas and influenced technical discussions, including architectural ones. It did not get the final vote on what AudioHole should become, which compromises were acceptable, or whether a proposed solution was worth shipping. Those decisions stayed with the person who has to live with the consequences.

And believe me, there were arguments. Lots of them. Nothing quite prepares you for debating an implementation detail with a machine that can produce three pages of impeccable technical prose while being spectacularly wrong about the one thing you asked it to check.

AI-generated code, explanations, and recommendations have been reviewed, challenged, revised, and tested throughout development. Some suggestions worked. Some needed serious surgery. Others went straight into the digital wood chipper. That process is ongoing, and this document is not a claim that every line has been independently audited or that the software is incapable of breaking in some breathtaking new way.

If AudioHole fails, I don't get to point at a chatbot and say the robot made me do it. The responsibility for what gets accepted, built, and released rests with the human directing the project. AI doesn't get to hold the screwdriver at the postmortem.

## Why use it?

Because the alternative was pretending that one independent developer ought to have encyclopedic knowledge of Windows audio, real-time programming, user-interface design, graphics, animation, build systems, and every peculiar little subsystem needed to make this thing work.

That would be an impressive delusion, even by my standards.

AI made it possible to explore unfamiliar territory faster, ask better questions, prototype ideas, and learn enough to make informed decisions. It helped bridge gaps that might otherwise have demanded a much larger team or several additional lifetimes spent browsing documentation written by people who apparently regard punctuation as a security vulnerability.

Useful? Absolutely. Infallible? Good God, no.

The trick is keeping your hands on the wheel. Know what you're trying to accomplish. Question the answers. Verify what you can. Test what you build. And when something smells wrong, follow the smell instead of accepting an explanation just because it came wrapped in confident technical vocabulary.

## On contributions

If you find a bug, spot a better approach, or have an idea worth trying, you're welcome to get involved. Use the tools that work for you. Write every line by hand while drinking cold coffee and cursing at the monitor, or vibe code the fuck out of it.

Either way, understand what you're submitting. Test it. Be upfront about meaningful limitations and unresolved problems. Make sure it plays nicely with the project's licenses and doesn't leave the next poor bastard digging through the wreckage of an inexplicable change.

I care a hell of a lot more about whether a contribution is understandable, maintainable, and works than whether its author personally typed every semicolon. Though if you introduce a mysterious timing regression and explain it with "the AI said it was fine," I reserve the right to stare at you through the screen.

**The ideas are human. The direction is human. The responsibility is human.**

The tools just helped us make the holes.
